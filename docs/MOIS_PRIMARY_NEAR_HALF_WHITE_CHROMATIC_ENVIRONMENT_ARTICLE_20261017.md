# Why is white so common? Global floral photographic colour balance across environmental gradients

**MOIS primary ecological Research Article — updated 18 October 2026. Not submitted.** This is a source-verified observational study and not an experimentally established pigment cost–fitness balance. Earlier integrated geographic-moisture-and-season draft remains historical context; seasonal climate results will be **Supplementary Information (SI)**, not a condition requiring within-individual seasonal flower-colour change.

## Abstract

White floral colour remains common worldwide despite the potential signalling, optical and protective functions of visible pigmentation. We ask not whether evolution maintains an exact 50:50 colour ratio, but **how widespread photographed white versus chromatic hues vary across water, irradiance, temperature and static habitat conditions**. We analysed **313,535 distinct original image-processed photographic attempts**, including **151,239 automatically classified flower-like four-hue ROIs**: **71,427 white-labelled (47.23%)** and **79,812 yellow/orange, red/pink or blue/purple combined**. Equal named-taxon and genus weighting gave descriptive white rates of **46.95%** and **50.08%**, respectively. This source is not a field census of flowering individuals, and unclassifiable image colour remains unknown.

In two observational precipitation×annual-irradiance tertile comparisons, photographed white frequencies ranged from **42.31%** in the dry/high-irradiance cell to **51.28%** in the wet/low-irradiance cell. A wet-minus-dry comparison across **19 hemisphere/10° latitude/solar strata** averaged **+2.51 percentage points**, with exploratory 95% stratum-bootstrap interval **[−0.32,+5.19]**, so a common conditional moisture direction is uncertain. In a novel **23,076-nominal-taxon/2,892-genus** geography- and genus-conditioned exploratory model, annual precipitation related positively to photographed white (+1.92 percentage points per original taxon-source SD, *nominal* genus-cluster interval [+0.15,+3.69]), whereas adjusted solar and the rainfall×solar and rainfall×temperature interactions had intervals spanning zero. Geography and annual climate explained only approximately **0.40%** of the genus-centred observed taxon-level white-photo variation. Matched-source soil and terrain blocks improved within-sample fit slightly, on distinct incompletely covered source subsets. An older frozen 309,439-photo six-axis maxT/999-genus model likewise supports overall annual water correlates but not a direction confirmed under coarse geographic conditioning.

Thus a globally near-half photographic composition masks differences across sampled environments, but does **not** identify the selective fitness benefits, pigment-production costs, or evolutionary maintenance of any fixed white–chromatic proportion. The main ecological question is which abiotic and biogeographic conditions accompany different colour compositions; observed-month seasonality remains Supplementary Information.

**Keywords:** floral hue; white flowers; flower colour; macroecology; moisture; solar radiation; environmental filtering; observer bias; iNaturalist

## Central research question

Why is visible white floral hue *common* rather than universally replaced by chromatic hues? A globally near-half descriptive source fraction invites analysis of **different ecological environments permitting or favouring different floral appearances**, rather than a postulated global trait frequency set point.

Competing contextual mechanisms—not conclusions from the photo census—include:
- Reduced cost of producing some **visible pigments** under some resource regimes; WHITE is never assumed pigment-free, and UV-absorbing phenolics and other compounds may persist.
- Potential visual attraction or signalling gains of nonwhite floral pigments in some pollination environments; pollinator assemblages and visitation were not measured for this global source.
- Photo- and thermal-optical consequences of visible hue, and possible ultraviolet/oxidative protection from molecules contributing to floral colour or co-occurring pigments.
- Evolutionary/taxonomic history, spatial turnover and different habitat occupancy, which can create an apparent near-half global photographic average without selection maintaining that value.
- Taxon inclusion, spatial and observer effort, photography, organ identity and hue-classifier missingness, all of which can shift the observed average.

No hypothesized benefit or cost was observed as fitness or biochemical flux. The source can **reject or qualify simple universal environmental-dominance hypotheses**, but cannot by itself establish stabilizing selection on white/chromatic morphs.

## Materials and methods: immutable measured pixels and nested outcomes

**Photo source.** The latest complete **313,535-source photo-pixel** cohort combines the previous 309,439 real original ROI-v4 photographs with all 16 successfully completed 256-photograph workers (4,096 genuine new original photo IDs, 1,577 new four-hue labels); the first 512 and 1,792 subset checkpoints are **not** separately counted. This yields **151,239 interpretable four-hue ROI labels**, including **71,427 white**, and **151,230** classified labels linked to original observation dates. The old 100,543-photo descriptive comparison was 21,574 white among 45,953 classifiable (46.95%), but it overlaps rather than independently replicating this source. The purposely season-gap-targeted separate 256-photo cohort is excluded from the primary global prevalence estimator. Colour-unclassifiable original photo attempts remain explicitly missing.

**Primary outcome.** Visible white among classifiable original ROIs, with yellow/orange, red/pink and blue/purple pooled as chromatic for the primary contrast. The three chromatic categories are also retained as distinct descriptive responses. Unclassifiable ROIs are not classified as nonwhite; all **313,535 actual image-pixel attempts** are the denominator for reporting classifiability and missingness. No photographed organ is assumed to be botanically validated as an open petal, no image visible hue assumed equivalent to biochemical anthocyanin expression.

**Different denominators.** Compute original photo-weighted white fraction; within nominal taxon photographed white fraction followed by equal taxon weighting; mean taxon fraction within genus then equal genus weighting; and equal 10° source-geographic cell summaries with at least 30 classifiable photographs. Cross-check genus minimum source taxa (1, 2, 5) and photo depth per taxon (1, 2, 5). The genus-replication percentile interval is descriptive uncertainty across sampled nominal genera, not a global adaptive-equilibrium test or full nonrandom source correction.

**Environment.** Use the newly fully source-verified **313,535-original-photo feature lake** (photographic colour/observation ID, annual WorldClim v2.1 BIO5/BIO12/BIO14/BIO15/solar and only-where-measured partial source soil/DEM terrain, each join SHA-verified by literal photo ID). Historic original WorldClim solar energy in kJ m^-2 day^-1 is already converted to W m^-2 by division by 86.4. **Do not fill soil/terrain for 4,096 new photo sites without actual raster values.** Annual climate normals are **1970–2000**, not photographed-year/month weather, and seasonal normals/weather comparisons remain SI. Retain the older 309,439-photo frozen six-axis maxT/999-genus model as a separate *prespecified source-model benchmark* rather than calling its coefficient a new independent replication.

**Analysis hierarchy.** Test:
1. Is white consistently *common* under photo/taxon/genus/geographic weights and classifiability controls? There is no null requiring exactly 0.5.
2. How does the photographic white proportion vary across annual precipitation, atmospheric vapour, warmest-month temperature, dry-month rain, rainfall seasonality and solar radiation?
3. How does any water relationship change after controlling for elevation, latitude, longitude, coarse cell/biogeographic scale, soil and DEM landscape terrain, and complete-case site support? Never confuse a change in the measured environmental population with evidence for a causal added predictor.
4. Do four separate hues vary differently, indicating that chromatic colour is not biologically uniform and not equivalent to anthocyanins alone?
5. Do original photographic classifiability and source recruitment bias plausibly alter white-versus-colour proportions?

Use original matched-photo/genus bootstrap maxT24 water inference as **already fitted** and retain its geographic nulls. On the latest complete 313,535-photo data, add all-photo rain×solar and rain×warmest-month-temperature 3×3 classifications, taxon/genus-equal ratios, and exploratory genus-centred multi-annual-climate and rain×solar/heat interaction models. Compare soil/terrain increments only on **identical complete-case taxon supports within each block**, and never subtract R² across source samples. Environmental deciles and nominal genus-cluster intervals are descriptive/exploratory beyond the earlier familywise maxT. Source date is not flower-opening verification, and an image hue is not a fitness or pigment-production observation.

## Source-authenticated main descriptive results

### Earlier 309,439-photo weighting sensitivity (not the latest 313,535-photo denominator)

| Photographic weighting/selection rule | White fraction | Interpretation |
|---|---:|---|
| All 149,662 classifiable photo ROI outcomes | 47.21% | Photo-weighted original hue source |
| Equal nominal taxon weights, 25,640 source taxa | 46.93% | Per-taxon mean of classified photo white outcomes |
| Equal nominal genus weights, 5,429 genera | 50.01% | Mean of equal-taxon photographic genus proportions; 95% genus-resampling interval 48.97–51.02% |
| Genus-equal, genus at least 2 photo-classifiable taxa | 48.66% | More taxonomically replicated genera |
| Genus-equal, genus at least 5 photo-classifiable taxa | 46.55% | More stringent genus species depth |
| Equal source-geographic 10° cells, min 30 classifiable photos, 207 cells | 46.90% | Cell weighting, **not** area-/flora-weighted |

These findings are intentionally **not** described as a maintained 50:50 equilibrium. The genus sensitivity shows how an apparent precisely half proportion changes with taxonomic source support. The primary inference is that white is a very substantial global visible-photo hue category, with no universal chromatic dominance in these observed images.

**Existing long-term water result:** original 309,439-photo genus-equal global annual precipitation +1.99 pp per environmental SD, maxT95 [+0.55,+3.42]; mean atmospheric vapour +3.16 [+1.04,+5.28]. With coarse geographic genus conditioning, precipitation +0.90 [−2.63,+4.44], vapour +2.15 [−2.64,+6.95]. The conditional simultaneous intervals include zero; no single universal water-to-white causal response is established.

### Environmental heterogeneity in the complete 313,535 measured-photo source

The observed full-source photo-white rate is **71,427 / 151,239 = 47.23%**, or 52.77% for the three chromatic visible-hue classes combined. The latest source had **162,296 original ROI attempts without an interpretable four-hue label**; these are not designated nonwhite. Source-taxon-equal and genus-equal photographic white averages are approximately **46.95%** and **50.08%**. The source-weighting/genera-with-many-taxa sensitivity table above is the older 309,439-photo benchmark and is **not recomputed from the complete fourth wave**.

Using environment terciles defined across **all** original photo attempts before hue filtering, observed white-labelled photo proportions varied along rainfall and annual irradiance:

| Annual precipitation | Lower solar | Middle solar | Higher solar |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Lower rainfall | **47.35%** | **45.81%** | **42.31%** |
| Middle rainfall | **49.70%** | **47.03%** | **42.72%** |
| Higher rainfall | **51.28%** | **51.14%** | **46.68%** |

The raw wet/low-solar versus dry/high-solar contrast is approximately **8.97 percentage points**, but this compares *different* sampled taxonomic/geographic/observer compositions, **not** directly measured adaptation or pigment savings. In the three solar bins, wetter-source photos are more often labelled white than drier-source photos. Distinguishing photograph-classification success and photo versus genus weights remains essential: for example, the wet/high-solar cell is **46.68% photo-weighted** but **51.31% equal-genus-weighted**; the dry/high-solar cell is **42.31% photo-weighted** versus **45.62% genus-weighted**. These are source-specific averaging rules, not competing biological truth values.

A latitude/solar partially stratified comparison containing **19 geographic-climate strata** and **92,400 classifiable original photographic observations** found a mean **wet−dry white photo contrast +2.51 pp**, exploratory 999-stratum-bootstrap 95% **[−0.32,+5.19]**. It does not require same locations, photographers or taxa inside these very broad strata and cannot identify a common causal moisture effect.

The exploratory genus-centred annual geography/climate fit contained **150,977 photographed colour rows** mapping to **23,076 named source taxa from 2,892 nominal genera** (the stricter congeneric source). Multivariate annual rainfall was **+1.92 pp per original predictor SD** (nominal genus-cluster 95% **[+0.15,+3.69]**); warmest-month temperature was **+1.42 [−0.01,+2.86]** and annual shortwave irradiance **−0.34 [−2.15,+1.47]**. The rainfall×solar interaction was **−0.42 [−1.78,+0.93]**, and rainfall×heat **+0.38 [−0.87,+1.63]** per product of standardised annual predictors; **neither interaction's nominal interval excludes zero**, and these tests have not been corrected across the full exploratory project history. Geography plus five annual climate terms explained only **0.0040 (0.40%) of within-genus taxon white-photo-rate variation**, and both interactions together increased in-sample R² by just **0.0000585**. Hence the visible composition is structured at an aggregate environmental level, but simple climate terms have very limited within-genus predictive explanatory ability here.

On **different, internally matched original complete-case populations**, adding DEM terrain to geography/climate increased within-sample R² by **0.00052** (104,809 classified photo rows, 20,639 qualifying taxa), and adding the originally measured historical soil properties increased it by **0.00175** (31,951 classified photo rows, 18,416 qualifying taxa). These are tiny observational fit differences on unequal soil/terrain source supports; they **cannot** be read as relative causal costs or site-level pigment mechanisms.

The complete source-replay, 18 rain×solar/heat cells, source-hue count and model terms are versioned in the [313,535-photo environmental-result release](https://github.com/zuizui0223/mois/releases/tag/mois-real313535-white-water-light-habitat-observational-v1). No seasonal climate regressions, flowering-anthesis truth set, experimental pigment biochemistry or fitness measurement was introduced.

## Discussion and limits

This is a phenomenon that invites explanation, not evidence that visible pigment is invariably costly, that white flowers have superior reproduction, or that balancing selection maintains a predetermined global percentage. Many white petals have nonvisible pigments or flavonoid functions; visible classifications are not chemical trait quantification. Colour may covary with pollinator biology, climate, geography and shared plant history. The existing strong source-location and classifiability filters, photo-identification uncertainty and missingness are necessary interpretive boundaries. Distinct localities and regions can have different colours while their aggregate appears close to half. The new moisture×solar results are consistent with contextual composition differences but, after geographic and nominal-genus adjustment, associated annual climate terms explain little taxon-level variation. This is compatible with multiple unmeasured mechanisms and taxonomic/photo selection; it neither demonstrates white-pigment metabolic savings nor that coloured flowers are universally selected against.
