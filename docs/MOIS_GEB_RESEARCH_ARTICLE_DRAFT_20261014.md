# The geographic and measurement dependence of flower-colour–moisture associations across a photographed global flora

**Working research article — 14 October 2026; provisional journal target: Global Ecology and Biogeography.** This is an **author-facing editable draft**, not yet anonymized for double-anonymous review. It is not submitted by this workflow. The author-identifying title page, coauthor review, final reference and figure checks, and permanent source archive remain outstanding. All analyses reported below are original FCP source-verified *retrospective observations*; migration to `mois` is not a new experiment or a new prospective replication.

**Running title:** Floral colour and moisture patterns

## Abstract

**Aim:** Floral colour–climate associations can reflect taxonomic turnover, ecological differences among congeners or uneven observation. We separate between-genus and within-genus photographed white–moisture gradients and examine their sensitivity to sampling and measurement.

**Location:** Worldwide georeferenced iNaturalist photographic opportunities, summarized globally and in four 90° longitude sectors.

**Time period:** Heterogeneous historical photographic records paired to archived long-term environmental summaries. Source dates are not sufficiently represented in the analysis-ready original-photo files for a temporal or contemporaneous flowering-weather test.

**Major taxa studied:** Named flowering-plant taxa; a classifiable four-state photographic hue was obtained for 45,953 of 100,543 unique images across an opportunity universe of 42,111 taxa.

**Methods:** We modelled four-colour photo fractions in 20,694 climate-complete species/1,934 genera using geographic controls and genus-resampled simultaneous intervals. We compared strict-quality and coarse-cell designs, held species fixed across spatial models, tested between/within-species slopes on identical images, and examined botanical-description and geographic-transfer results.

**Results:** In 20,694 species/1,934 genera, photographed white increased with rainfall within genera (+2.255 percentage points/source SD; 24-comparison simultaneous 95% interval +0.319 to +4.190). On exactly the same species, the geographically adjusted between-genus rainfall slope was +8.555 (ordinary genus-bootstrap 95% interval +6.220 to +10.786), with a positive paired between-minus-within difference. Both scales were also positive for atmospheric vapour. Strict-image and local coarse-cell models did not independently confirm the worldwide direction, and a matched-photo within-species test did not show the same positive slope.

**Main conclusions:** Genus-mean and congeneric white–moisture gradients coexist, but climatic covariates barely predict differences between unseen congeneric species. Photographic selection, geographic structure and unmeasured fitness prevent causal pigment-trade-off conclusions.

**Keywords:** citizen science, climate, floral colour, geographic sampling, macroecology, measurement bias, precipitation, trait variation

## 1 | Introduction

Visible flower colour is among the most recognizable reproductive plant traits, yet a floral colour seen in a photograph can encode multiple biological and observational processes. Species differ in pigments, petal structures, pollinator environments and evolutionary histories, while site-specific illumination, image selection, flower-organ detection and camera exposure alter what can be classified from an opportunistically submitted photograph. Separating these scales matters when a trait–climate association is interpreted as evidence for climatic adaptation.

Abiotic correlates of floral colour are biologically plausible. Light and thermal conditions can alter floral optical functions, and drought or nutrient environments might change the relative investments and benefits of pigments. Pollinator distributions and local plant-community structure may also covary with climate. In a quantitative analysis of 339 species, Dalrymple et al. (2020) reported that flower colour was associated with pollinator and plant assemblages as well as precipitation and solar radiation. On Malesian islands, a separate botanical-trait and phylogenetic study found more white-flowered taxa in wet tropical lowlands while floral-colour distributions also depended on insularity and temperature (Trethowan et al., 2024). These precedents mean that simply identifying white flowers in wet places would not, by itself, establish a new general biological mechanism.

A large photographic atlas adds geographical and taxonomic coverage, but it does not automatically resolve why a pattern appears. Original image colour is a conditional response: a labelled photo is not a reflectance spectrum or authenticated floral pigment, and only photos whose floral region and visible colour can be segmented reliably enter a four-hue analysis. Community-science observation behaviour is spatially and taxonomically nonrandom (Di Cecco et al., 2021). If the chance of obtaining a classifiable photo varies with climate and floral appearance, both the apparent geographical prevalence of white and its climatic gradient may be distorted. Similarly, analyses at a worldwide genus-adjusted scale can combine widely separated congeneric species that are not comparable within one local plant assemblage.

The distinction between a **between-species** and a **within-species** colour–climate relation is especially important. The same global association might result from historical lineage composition, niche distribution or developmental constraints, without predicting that white-flowered photographs of the same named species occur more frequently at wetter sites. Botanical reflectance studies at the population level show how climatic and colonization-history correlates can differ for components of floral colour (Koski & Galloway, 2020). A positive species-level slope is therefore not a selection coefficient and should not automatically be projected onto individuals or local breeding populations.

We address three connected questions using archived source-measured photographs and deliberately different statistical tests. **First**, is there a detectable direction linking visible white, relative to yellow/orange, red/pink and blue/purple, with rainfall and atmospheric water-vapour gradients among nominal congeneric taxa globally? **Second**, do the signed coefficients survive image-quality restrictions, alternative geographic conditioning of identical species, and botanical-colour verification? **Third**, where does joint environmental predictive information persist when entire genera and increasingly distant geographic support are excluded, and does the signed rain–white relation reappear within species on exactly matched photographic evidence?

Our primary estimand is **photographed taxon-level floral-colour composition conditional on observation and classification**, not genetic white-morph incidence, petal UV reflectance or plant reproductive fitness. The methods and interpretations are explicitly retrospective. We use both positive and negative source-verified endpoints to delimit the scale on which a global result can be generalized, rather than selecting the most supportive region, colour category or model after inspecting its outcome.

## 2 | Materials and Methods

### 2.1 | Source photographs, unique image identities and visible colour measurement

The original photographic opportunity census included one breadth image per nominal species and additional species-by-geographic-cell records from iNaturalist. Specifically, the source programme held 42,111 one-photo taxon opportunities and 85,337 taxon–cell photographic records, with **26,905 duplicate photo identifiers** between the two sets. Deduplication by original photo ID therefore yielded **100,543 unique photographs from 42,111 nominal named species**. The selection was based on photographed flowering records with source taxon, observation and photo identifiers and georeferences. Species opportunity and photo availability are not probability samples of the world flora.

An original fixed visible-colour measurement machine used ROI-v4 to detect a floral region and EfficientSAM (Xiong et al., 2024) to segment a flower mask. Segmented pixels were measured against the frozen nine-element visible palette (white, yellow, orange, red, pink, magenta, purple, blue and bronze), from which four coarse photo hue labels were assigned under explicit classification gates. A coarse photo label could be **white**, **yellow/orange**, **red/pink** or **blue/purple**. Exactly **45,953** photographs had an admissible four-colour label; **54,590** had no classifiable label. Unknown colours were not coded as white, coloured, or biologically monomorphic. Segmented flower pixels also allowed nine-palette continuous white-area sensitivity analyses on a restricted source subset. This image system is neither wavelength-calibrated spectrophotometry, UV reflectance nor an anthocyanin assay. Original classifier artefacts and near-clipping remain possible explanations for apparent white categories.

We copied the archived photographic outcome files without relabelling them. The archived raw-photo bundle is identified by SHA256 `2d6097ba1b0434632e13bbe79dd78180608a287ff5dabebc770b5c0a2cfb52a0`. The original broad/region source is photographed opportunity; it does not establish genetically distinct morphs, independent individual plants, or year-specific flowering phenotype distributions.

### 2.2 | Geographic and environmental join; source denominators

The photographic source was joined one-to-one to original-site environmental records using **taxon ID, observation ID and photo ID simultaneously**, rejecting unmatched or duplicated original identifiers. The original spatial predictors were derived from WorldClim climate surfaces (Fick & Hijmans, 2017) and SoilGrids soil-property surfaces (Poggio et al., 2021), according to the archived FCP environmental-source specification; the joined archive is authoritative for exact source layer choices. Its separately archived environmental ZIP SHA256 is `6e287294b3a05eaa383be3de644551825915bfecca9c4bde6950b4890f5e16ca`. The 36 abiotic variables comprise temperature (11 bioclimatic variables), precipitation (eight), solar radiation (two), wind (two), vapour pressure (two), elevation (one) and soil properties (ten). We treated them as correlated environmental covariates, not independent causal interventions.

With valid source coordinates, known colour and complete 26-variable nonsoil environmental support, **45,836 original photos representing 25,028 taxa** were eligible. Including all ten soil predictors reduced the separate complete-case panel to **31,925 photographs from 20,786 taxa**. Restricting subsequent directional regressions to nominal genera with at least three distinct sampled taxa yielded **20,694 species in 1,934 genera** for climate26 and **16,801 species in 1,665 genera** for soil36. These are *different eligibility rules applied within different source panels*; the soil36 count is not simply an attrition of 20,694 taxa.

Photographic site latitude and longitude were transformed into absolute latitude and circular sine/cosine longitude nuisance covariates. A coarse sensitivity grid has 18 longitude bins and nine equal-area-in-sine-latitude bins, with 162 possible cell IDs. This supports comparison at a broad taxonomic–geographic grain, not matched microsites, contemporaneous humidity measurements or a fitted fine-scale spatial random field.

### 2.3 | Estimating signed colour–environment slopes among congeners

For every qualified species, photographed class probabilities were defined as the observed four-colour photo fractions; its predictor values and nuisance coordinates were source-photo-site averages. Species thus contributed one averaged photographic-composition row rather than a number of independent observations proportional to photographic effort.

We assessed a retrospective family of six interpretable environmental axes: warmest-month maximum temperature (BIO5), annual precipitation (BIO12), driest-month precipitation (BIO14), precipitation seasonality (BIO15), annual mean solar radiation and annual mean atmospheric vapour pressure. An additional soil available-water proxy was included where all soil predictors were complete. All axes were standardized to original-source environmental standard deviations rather than rescaled separately for a selected region or quality subset. Coefficients are therefore expressed in **percentage points of originally classified photographic hue probability per original-source environmental SD**.

Within nominal genera, we centred predictors and photo-colour fractions and adjusted each focal environment for absolute latitude and circular longitude. On identical eligible species, we separately regressed genus-mean photo-white against genus-mean moisture and geographic coordinates, weighting each genus once; paired genus bootstrap compared those slopes with within-genus estimates. This distinguishes statistical scales, not biological causes. This is not a single simultaneously fitted model with all six correlated ecological axes: individual signed-axis contrasts condition on the three broad geographic nuisance coordinates, and a separate all26/all36 ridge model gives descriptive partial coefficients. We report both species-equal and genus-equal weighting, emphasizing genus-equal worldwide contrasts to avoid assigning large genera disproportionate total influence.

The source retains 20 photo/population/location scopes, five regions (world and four 90° longitude sectors), strict versus ordinary photographic quality, and different local/group adjustments. The strict original image gate required at least **23,826 effective flower pixels** and nonflower nuisance fraction no greater than **0.1375717742**. Source-based whole-genus resampling used **999 bootstrap draws**, shared across all hue/environment contrasts within a scope. Approximate maxT simultaneous 95% intervals protect the **six (or seven) axes × four hues within one population/weight/scope**, not the many choices of region, eligibility rule, model family or hypotheses across the broader exploration. Positive support in one model must not be described as study-wide correction or as a prospectively fixed treatment effect. Figure 1 summarizes these original source-conditional contrasts and their uncertainty.

### 2.4 | Matched-species spatial-conditioning analysis

To separate changes in species support from changes in geographic adjustment, we used the already completed three-stage source analysis in the world and eastern (90–180° E) sectors. Stage A keeps all species from genera that can qualify for the same-genus×cell comparison while retaining broad coordinate controls. Stage B retains species in groups with at least three different congeners in the relevant coarse cell, retaining the **same broad coordinate model**. Stage C uses **exactly the stage B species, photo outcomes and environmental means**, but instead centres within genus-by-coarse-cell groups. Thus A–B mainly changes eligible source species and geographical opportunity; B–C changes statistical conditioning on an identical taxon set. C does not preserve the original same single-species covariate contrast, and B–C cannot be read as the causal effect of geography.

For the genus-equal headline B/C comparisons, the world-matched denominator was **9,370 taxa /845 genera /14,268 photos** and the eastern denominator **2,886 taxa /280 genera /4,149 photographs**. Stage differences used **999 identical whole-genus resampling draws** and familywise approximate maxT intervals for six axes × four hues separately in each region and weighting. These are matched species/estimated responses, not independent geographic plant replicates.

### 2.5 | Prediction, taxonomic transfer and geographic transfer

Joint environmental information was evaluated separately from the sign of any single BIO12 coefficient. The original full climate26 and soil36 models compared held-out multiclass four-hue squared-error (Brier) loss against geography/taxonomic baselines under fixed, source-defined geographic or whole-genus folds. A **positive gain** denotes smaller held-out error from adding environment. The climate26 and soil36 complete-case datasets score different species and must not be subtracted for a soil effect.

Other source-verified prediction analyses, retained as sensitivity rather than new primary inferential families, hold out entire genera; decompose the error of species-colour composition into genus-mean and within-genus species-deviation terms using the *same model predictions*; and exclude training species in the test longitude sector and within 500 km of test-source photos. These progressively restricted geographical designs change training-flora and environmental support, so a prediction drop is not uniquely due to spatial causation.

A subsequent source-exposed test used one fixed ExtraTrees learner (128 trees, maximum depth 16 and minimum leaf 20) on the **same originally tested taxa and folds**. Its full-environment prediction is compared with a geography-only baseline and with the original ridge full model. An environmental improvement over a *weaker nonlinear geographic baseline* is not sufficient to claim that nonlinear modelling solved geographic transfer.

### 2.6 | Identical-original-photo between/within-species comparison

A complementary original-photo analysis holds the same **21,448 photo IDs from 6,143 species and 787 genera** between a congeneric species-level and a within-named-species photo-level fit for the originally classified white response. Both use archived BIO12 and BIO1/BIO5/BIO15, elevation and broad geographic covariates, under their respective level-specific centring/conditioning. BIO12 is standardized by its source-photo SD of approximately **736.416 mm**. The original **1,999 paired genus-bootstrap draws** quantify their difference. Comparing slopes on identical photo outcomes reduces one source of denominator confounding, but the models still target different statistical variations; their difference is not an evolutionary selection contrast. The analysis was exploratory and was not pre-frozen to test this study's main manuscript.

A further original sensitivity applies continuous segmented white-palette fraction (not a coarse white label) to **14,667 photo IDs from 3,351 species**. It uses a different original-source BIO12 SD (**717.253 mm**), so its coefficient cannot be averaged directly with the categorical analysis. Strict floral ROI quality changes the eligible species set. No independent photographed-organ colour standards were collected for this comparison.

### 2.7 | Missing-colour selection, independent phenotype descriptions and mechanistic limits

Among the 100,543 original photos, the classifiability gate excludes 54.3%. Source wet/dry photographic opportunity deciles were defined across *all source photos* before checking their floral hue. We report four-state white shares only conditional on successful classification and retain a missing-not-at-random (MNAR) tipping exercise for the unknown colours. This sensitivity treats unclassified dry photo-white prevalence as an assumption and asks what unmeasured prevalence among unclassified wet photos would erase the apparent wet–dry white difference. The unobserved colour proportions are not data-driven estimates. We also computed sharp wet–dry bounds by hypothetically classifying all missing photo hues as white or nonwhite; these are not confidence intervals.

An external phenotype comparison uses Natural History Museum botanical flower/petal/corolla colour descriptions, retaining only strict and noncontradictory white and blue/purple values under matching named species. The independent-source **phenotypic** evidence retains original FCP photographed localities for climate/site covariates. Thus this is not an independent occurrence/climate study, and its binary white–blue response is not numerically exchangeable with the four-category photographed response. No data set records morph genotypes, flower pigment masses, UV reflectance, pollinator gains, reproductive fitness or temporally matched climate treatments.

### 2.8 | Independent verification using recovered original source tables

Both exact historical FCP source ZIPs were **recovered and byte-SHA256 verified** in a separate working execution environment, with ZIP integrity checks. They contain photo-colour measurements, source photo/observation identifiers, site locations and environmental tables, **not the original camera image pixels**. An independent source-data reader joined taxon, observation and photo IDs one-to-one; replicated climate26 and soil36 source denominators; and reconstructed all four hue point coefficients for original-source BIO12 precipitation and mean vapour pressure in 20,694 eligible species, independently of the historical FCP regression implementation. All **eight coefficients** agreed with the frozen 1,280-row ledger to a maximum absolute discrepancy of **3.51×10⁻¹⁴ percentage points**. That first audit verified point coefficients only. **A second, original-source computation reconstructed the full 999-replicate, six-environment-axis × four-colour (24-contrast) genus-bootstrap family for the world climate26 genus-equal model**, using anonymous per-genus sufficient statistics. Its 24 point estimates, percentile bootstrap confidence intervals, and within-family maxT 95% intervals and support indicators matched the original frozen ledger (maximum absolute difference **3.55×10⁻¹⁵ percentage points**). This does not reproduce every other model/region family or independently validate original camera-pixel classification; the sufficient-statistic intermediate remains restricted pending licence/geoprivacy review. The aggregate-only receipt contains original licence/coordinate-source status counts, but no individual photographed records are deposited in `mois` (Supporting Information S6).

### 2.9 | Revalidation of identical-species geographic comparisons

An additional independent source-data implementation reconstituted the original **576 stage-and-difference values** for global and eastern original-photo support, each under species-equal and genus-equal weighting. From the same exact SHA256-recovered original photo and environmental tables, this implementation reconstructed stage A/B/C species sets, fitted separate broad-versus-genus×coarse-cell normal equations, and reran **999 paired whole-genus bootstrap draws** with the original scope seeds. It compared **576 points, 1,152 ordinary interval endpoints and 576 paired-difference within-family maxT endpoints** directly with the original frozen source rows. It contains no new source-colour outcomes or causal geographical intervention; the paired maxT controls 24 environmental-axis × hue tests **within** each original region/weight/contrast, not the historical programme of model/region selection (Supporting Information S6).

### 2.10 | Independent re-estimation of strict-image, genus-by-cell and soil36 uncertainty

Using the same two original SHA256-verified measured photo/site source ZIPs, a further independent regression and genus-bootstrap implementation reproduced three separate worldwide, genus-equal response families: **strict photographic quality climate26** (5,848 species/727 genera), **climate26 within-genus×coarse-cell** (9,370/845), and **soil36-complete broad geography** (16,801/1,665). For each we re-estimated original source photo-colour means, geographic predictors where specified, the six or seven signed ecological axes, and **999 original-seed genus bootstrap replicates**. All 76 original point coefficients, ordinary percentile intervals and separately within-family maxT intervals were compared directly against the 1,280-row frozen original results; no new environment/hue test family was selected (S6).

### 2.11 | Exploratory single-genus influence diagnostic

To check whether either principal worldwide photographic white–moisture coefficient depended on one highly influential genus, we used the existing 1,934 source-genus normal-equation crossproducts to remove **one qualifying nominal genus at a time**. The original genus-equal, three-coordinate-plus-one-climate regression and source-global environmental scaling were held fixed. This generated 1,934 deletion refits per focal axis (BIO12 annual rainfall and mean vapour pressure), each estimating all four photographic hues. These leave-one-genus-out point ranges are **retrospective influence diagnostics**, not additional independent samples, hypothesis tests or re-estimated confidence intervals (Supporting Information S6).

## 3 | Results

### 3.1 | Photographic colour availability and conditional worldwide white prevalence

Among 100,543 source photos, **45,953 (45.7%)** had a classifiable four-hue label and **54,590 (54.3%)** did not; **21,574 (46.95%)** classified photos were white. The full-photo white share is only bounded at **21.46–75.75%**; exact **50:50** would require 52.57% white among unclassified photos. Source species-equal and genus-equal white values were **46.82%** (25,064 classifiable taxa) and **49.89%** (5,369 genera; **46.63%** with ≥5 classified species/genus), not evolutionary equilibria (Fig. 5a). Across **1,935 genera** containing ≥3 sampled taxa, between-genus composition accounted for **34.4%** of taxon-level photographed-white variation, versus **9.3%** under genus-size-preserving taxon-fraction permutations (S2).

Photo classification was not geographically neutral. In the driest original-photo rainfall decile, 4,981 of 10,084 source photos were classifiable (**49.4%**), compared with 3,904 of 9,990 (**39.1%**) in the wettest. Among classifiable photos, white was **41.80%** versus **53.25%**, an unadjusted wet–dry contrast of **+11.45 percentage points** (not comparable with the genus-adjusted coefficient). The MNAR tipping exercise showed that, if 50% of the unclassified dry photographs were truly white, a wet unclassified white fraction of **41.26%** would eliminate a *latent all-photo* wet/dry difference. No such missing-photo true hue was measured (Fig. 2; Table 1). Allowing all missing hues to be white or nonwhite gives hypothetical all-photo white shares of **20.65–71.25%** (dry) and **20.81–81.73%** (wet): a wet–dry difference of **−50.44 to +61.09 percentage points**. These logical bounds do not apply to the adjusted congeneric slope (S2).

### 3.2 | Broad congeneric photographic white–moisture directions are positive

In the climate26 global genus-equal panel (**20,694 species, 1,934 genera**), annual precipitation BIO12 was associated with **+2.255 percentage points of photographed white per global photo-site environmental SD**, within-scope 24-colour–axis maxT 95% interval **[+0.319,+4.190]**. Mean annual atmospheric vapour pressure yielded **+3.952 points [1.199,6.704]**. A soil36 analysis on a separately defined eligible set (**16,801 species, 1,665 genera**) gave BIO12 white **+2.257 [0.138,4.376]**; these two broad coefficients involve different underlying complete-case source taxa.

The world genus-equal BIO12 point coefficients for yellow/orange (**−1.221**), red/pink (**−0.446**) and blue/purple (**−0.588**) collectively offset white by construction of the four-colour composition, but each of their **individual 24-contrast maxT 95% intervals spans zero**. Similarly, for water vapour the three nonwhite hue points (−1.385, −1.509 and −1.057) have intervals spanning zero. This is evidence for a source-conditional white-versus-combined-nonwhite compositional contrast, **not** three independently confirmed coloured-hue decreases (Fig. 4).

Original within-design 24-maxT estimates were independently recovered from the source ZIPs (S6), without new biological replication.

With rainfall, vapour and elevation jointly fitted, both conditional white slopes remained positive but their two-contrast maxT intervals included zero. The standardized shared-moisture direction remained positive (**+3.644 pp**, two-contrast 95% **[+1.137,+6.152]**), without identifying an independent causal driver (S2).

All 1,934 leave-one-genus-out checks retained positive white–rainfall and white–vapour slopes, without identifying biological selection (S6).

On exactly these species, the genus-mean white/BIO12 slope was **+8.555 pp/SD** (ordinary genus-bootstrap 95% **[+6.220,+10.786]**) versus within-genus **+2.255 [+1.030,+3.563]**; the paired difference was **+6.300 [+3.666,+8.779]**. For vapour, between-genus **+9.370** and within-genus **+3.952** were both positive (Fig. 5b; S2). Genus contributions were mixed: 830 positive, 719 negative and 385 photo-white-invariant for BIO12; among colour-variable genera, 53.6% contributed positively (Fig. 5c; S2). These contrasting scales are not a causal attribution of the pooled association. BIO5 and solar-radiation white intervals were inconclusive. Because multiple original scopes and families were explored, these source-conditional maxT intervals cannot certify a *universal* moisture–colour rule.

### 3.3 | Image and within-genus local geography restrict the signed pattern

Under the strict photograph-quality gate, climate26 white/BIO12 was **+1.229 [−2.046,+4.504]**; within genus-by-coarse-geographic-cell comparison it was **+0.808 [−3.556,+5.172]**. Both simultaneous 95% intervals include zero. Their smaller or differently distributed source populations mean these observations bound robustness but do not prove the full-population effect vanished when simply changing one imaging or geographic variable (Fig. 1b; Table 2). Both complete 24-axis×hue families were independently re-estimated from the source ZIPs with **999 whole-genus resamples**: **no** original strict-photo or genus×cell coefficient had a within-family maxT interval excluding zero. The full 48 points, ordinary intervals and maxT intervals matched the original ledger to **4.44×10⁻¹⁶ percentage points** (S6).

In matched-species spatial analyses, the world sample contained 9,370 species/845 genera. Under broad coordinate adjustment (stage B), white/BIO12 was **+1.326**, while genus×cell centring on the **same species** (stage C) yielded **+0.808**. In 2,886 eastern species/280 genera, B was **+1.102** versus C **−2.076**. The eastern B−C paired difference was **+3.178 points** with nominal bootstrap 95% interval [+1.077,+5.351], but its **24-contrast simultaneous interval [−0.258,+6.613]** includes zero. The matching establishes that the *model specification*, not only the number of eligible species, can change the source estimate; it does not demonstrate a climate effect removed by spatial control (Fig. 1c; Table 2). The independent re-implementation reproduced **all 576 archived original spatial point estimates, all 1,152 nominal interval endpoints and all 576 matched-difference maxT interval endpoints** from the original ZIPs (maximum absolute error **1.60×10⁻¹⁴ percentage points**). For the world genus-equal B−C white/BIO12 coefficient, the corrected paired 95% interval also included zero (**+0.518 [−1.254,+2.289]**), as did the eastern comparison (S6).

### 3.4 | Same original photo colours do not reveal a shared positive within-species rainfall slope

An original photo-identity matched set of **21,448 images from 6,143 taxa and 787 genera** yielded between-congeneric photographed-white/BIO12 **+2.722 points per source SD** (paired genus-bootstrap 95% [+0.705,+4.714]), but within-named-species **−0.511** (95% [−1.436,+0.335]). The source-exploratory difference, between minus within, was **+3.233 [1.175,5.400]**; original one-sided paired bootstrap *P* = 0.0005. The signed within-species slope is not supported as positive. This is a between-/within-statistical-level difference on identical original labels, **not** a difference in selection strength or a new independent image cohort.

A continuous-photo-white ROI fraction analysis on 14,667 source photographs/3,351 species likewise estimated a positive congeneric coefficient **+2.229 points** (95% [0.177,4.282]) but unsupported within-species **−0.180** (95% [−1.011,+0.651]). Restricting source ROI quality weakened the congeneric coefficient to **+1.116 [−1.169,+3.401]**. Thus the between-/within distinction remains an important observational result, but its imaging and selection sensitivity precludes interpreting it as verified pigment biology (Fig. 2c; Table 2).

### 3.5 | Joint environmental prediction is small and strongly support-dependent

Earlier source heldout models on different climate26/soil36 cohorts had Brier gains **+0.002943** and **+0.001432**, which cannot identify soil's increment. A matched 20,786-species analysis gave soil10 increment **+0.0000757 [−0.000516,+0.000653]**. On another 16,801-species soil36 panel, the original white–soil available-water **proxy** coefficient was **+1.698 pp/SD** (original maxT95 **[+0.0028,+3.393]**).

On the **same 16,801 species**, rainfall/vapour improved unseen-genus photo-white prediction over geography (**+0.002355**); soil5 alone also had a positive but uncertain gain (**+0.001032 [−0.000012,+0.001997]**). Moisture2 outperformed soil5 by **+0.001323 [+0.000436,+0.002214]**, but adding rainfall/vapour to the other 24 climate axes gave **+0.000040 [−0.000141,+0.000226]**. Soil5 added to the full26 climate block worsened prediction by **−0.000404**, with 31/32 negative settings across ridge/fold sensitivity (soil10: 32/32). These source-specific results suggest shared climatic information, not a soil-free ecological mechanism (S2).

All36 unseen-genus prediction improved absolute photo-colour over geography (**+0.004518 [0.002437,0.006602]**, 20,786 species), but not within-genus species deviations (**−0.000142 [−0.000748,+0.000433]**). Its fitted-loss decomposition assigned improvement to genus means (+0.004699) rather than residuals (−0.000181); neither is causal ecological variance.

On 16,801 soil36 taxa, 13 predictors explained **0.6202%** of genus-centred photo-white variation. In unseen-genus **congeneric pairs** (no test-genus colour mean for prediction), rainfall/vapour reduced geography-only squared error from **0.316285 to 0.315620** (**0.21%**, **+0.000665 [−0.000227,+0.001506]**). All four genus-split intervals crossed zero; soil5's gain changed sign (S2).

After excluding genera and source-flora footprints in the test longitude sector and within 500 km, all36 ridge environmental gain fell to **+0.000174 [−0.002629,+0.002846]**. A fixed nonlinear model had a positive environmental increment over its *own* geography-only model (**+0.004882 [0.002320,0.007496]**), but its geography-only baseline performed worse than the ridge geography-only baseline, and the direct nonlinear versus ridge **full-model** advantage remained uncertain (**+0.001202 [−0.001011,+0.003447]**). Strict-quality nonlinear environmental gain **+0.000517 [−0.004122,+0.005363]** was inconclusive (Table S2). These are prediction-model results, not tests of adaptive flowering responses. Figure 3 makes the changes in prediction target, geographic source support and model baseline explicit.

### 3.6 | Botanical-description checks do not independently confirm the direction

The strict botanical-description white versus blue/purple comparison among **670 named species in 58 genera** in the eastern sector estimated genus-equal rainfall contrast **+3.56 percentage points** with ordinary 95% interval **[−15.34,+22.60]**. Its direction was imprecise and the interval includes zero. Botanical hue labels were independent of FCP's photographic classifier, but climate and site support remained derived from the original photo locations and the binary trait denominator differed. The result therefore cannot be used as either a conclusive falsification or independent confirmation of the worldwide four-colour association (Fig. 1d).

## 4 | Discussion

### 4.1 | What is repeated worldwide, and at what scale?

Our principal finding is the coexistence of **positive genus-mean and within-genus species-level white–moisture associations** on identical photographed taxa, alongside uncertain geographic and photographic portability. Since the probabilities of white and three coloured classes sum to one, increasing photographic white implies an aggregate nonwhite decrease, not significant decreases in each coloured hue; Figure 4 and original four-hue maxT intervals make this distinction explicit. Both congeneric white–moisture slopes remained positive in genera with contrasting sampled sunlight, peak temperature and rainfall (S2), without identifying a physiological pigment trade-off. Its sign remained unchanged after individually removing any one of 1,934 source genera, but this single-genus influence stability neither establishes a phylogenetically universal mechanism nor repairs nontransport under tighter geography and image quality. That result extends taxonomic and geographical photographic sampling beyond earlier small regional colour–climate analyses. However, both prior floral macroecology (Dalrymple et al., 2020) and work on species-rich Malesian islands (Trethowan et al., 2024) already associate flower-colour traits with abiotic environments. Scale, measurement support and a transparent record of failed transfers—not a newly discovered expectation that wet regions have white flowers—are the distinctive contributions available from these data.

At the ecological scale, genus-mean rain gradients were larger than congeneric gradients on identical taxa, yet rainfall/vapour cut congeneric pairwise loss by only **0.21%** versus geography in a **different soil-complete cohort** (S2). These are different estimands, not a joint variance partition or proof of adaptation. The worldwide congeneric slope also aggregates opposing genus contributions; shared evolutionary history, floristic composition and photograph selection remain viable explanations. The unsupported within-species photo-rainfall slope identifies no mechanism. Detailed FCP within-species analyses are separate.

### 4.2 | Geographic conditioning is not a biological intervention

Matched species sets show that shifts between broad and genus×cell adjustments cannot be attributed *solely* to taxon exclusion: the models retain different geographic variation. The eastern difference's simultaneous interval includes zero and cannot identify local climate selection. Distant-genus and geographical prediction results likewise fail to demonstrate a portable water–white mechanism; a nonlinear model's environmental gain depended on its own weaker geographic baseline (S3–S4).

Even on identical species, moisture overlaps with geography. The photographed-white/elevation coefficient changed from **−1.90 to +1.01 points per 1,000 m** after including atmospheric vapour; the absolute-latitude coefficient increased by **+2.60 points per 10°**. These shifts follow exactly from genus-equal weighted-regression covariate algebra (S2). They do **not** show causal vapour mediation, independent altitude adaptation or pigment photoprotection.

### 4.3 | Photographic missingness is an inferential problem, not a footnote

A majority of original photo opportunities lacked a classifiable four-colour phenotype. Wet and dry source deciles differed substantially in classifier success. Such missingness cannot be cured by merely counting more downloaded images; the classifier outcome itself may depend on lighting, flower type, background, observer preferences and habitat. The MNAR tipping exercise is important because a feasible difference in **unknown** photo-colour proportions could erase the latent wet–dry gradient even where observed-classified contrast is strong. Inverse-probability weighting cannot test its missing-at-random assumption; the unrestricted latent wet–dry difference remains unidentified.

Photo-white labels also do not imply chemically pigment-free petals, a particular UV absorption pattern or bee-perceived achromatic colour. Some white photographed flowers may contain nonvisual flavonoids; some colourful petals may appear white under exposure or ROI mislocalization. Expert botanical descriptions mitigate one measurement channel but are much smaller, qualitatively coarser, and paired with the original photo-site geography. Without calibrated organ-specific measurements from independent sites, conclusions must remain framed as **observed digital flower colours**.

### 4.4 | Competing explanations and suitable future tests

The photographic patterns motivate several **competing**, not mutually exclusive, hypotheses. Drier environments may favour protective pigmentation or alter floral thermal environments; wetter communities may differ in pollinators or coloured-lineage species pools; local soil and vegetation may confound rain and visible floral hue. This genus-level photographic clustering could reflect lineage history, ecological sorting or shared classification biases, without establishing phylogenetic conservatism, frequency-dependent selection or pigment fitness benefits. The source lacks actual pollen/seed relative fitness, pigment concentrations, plant genotypes and known evolutionary transitions. A source spatial-composition test found no negative between-/within-species compensation (one-sided *P*=0.997); global photographic near-parity therefore does not identify balancing selection.

A next-stage experiment must compare morph-verified genotypes within well-sampled populations under controlled water treatments and record pigment chemistry, pollinator responses and both seed and pollen success. A distinct macroecological follow-up should obtain independent, standardized botanical/spectrometric flower-colour observations with original **non-FCP** occurrence coordinates and revisit the **same**, prespecified BIO12 comparison across source regions. Such designs could distinguish trait measurement error from biogeographical filtering and selection, unlike scanning more weather axes in the same archived photo outcomes.

## 5 | Main conclusions

Among 20,694 climatically complete, genus-qualified photographed taxa, white visible colour was associated with more annual precipitation and atmospheric water vapour under broad genus/geographic adjustment. The association was less well identified in strict-image, local congeneric and independent botanical-colour comparisons, and the same photographed hue outcomes did not demonstrate a common positive within-species rainfall slope. These results support a **scale- and measurement-conditional macroecological pattern**, not a universal climate-colour law, selection coefficient or pigment cost/benefit trade-off. Testing the latter will require source-independent measurements at the level of floral organs, individuals and populations.
